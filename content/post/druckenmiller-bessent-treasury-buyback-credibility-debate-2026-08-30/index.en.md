The 30-year U.S. Treasury yield reached 5.31% on August 17, its highest level in 19 years. Two days later, the Treasury announced that it would raise the maximum size of buyback operations in the 10-to-30-year sectors from $2 billion to at least $4 billion per operation.

Five days after the announcement, Stanley Druckenmiller objected publicly. He argued that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent, his former Soros Fund colleague and protégé, was no longer managing liquidity. In Druckenmiller's view, Treasury was trying to manage the price of long-term government debt.

The visible dispute is about a small $4 billion operation. The real dispute is much larger: <strong>how far can a government manage the world's benchmark long-term interest rate</strong>, and when does a higher yield reflect broken market plumbing rather than a fiscal warning?

## TL;DR

- Treasury will raise the long-end buyback cap from $2 billion to at least $4 billion per operation from September 9 through November 4. No operation has yet occurred under the enlarged terms.
- The original program has produced a measurable liquidity benefit. An IMF study found that eligibility reduced bid-ask spreads on targeted off-the-run Treasuries by about 0.2 basis point. The effect is small but real.
- Druckenmiller has the stronger case in this specific dispute. There was limited evidence of acute market dysfunction before the announcement, while Bessent explicitly discussed the policy's signaling value and said prevailing yields did not reflect fundamentals.
- Treasury buybacks are not Federal Reserve quantitative easing. Treasury does not create reserves. It uses cash or other debt issuance to retire older securities, changing the maturity and liquidity mix rather than erasing the debt stock.
- A Treasury backstop may slow a disorderly rise in long yields. It is unlikely to change their direction for long unless the fiscal deficit and inflation credibility improve. For Korean equities, the 10-year real yield and demand at 20- and 30-year auctions matter more than the buyback headline alone.

The buyback tool itself is not the problem. Rules-based purchases of illiquid off-the-run bonds can improve market functioning. The character of the tool changes when Treasury doubles the program immediately after yields spike and begins to signal a view on the correct level of long-term rates. ## 1. The dispute began with a 5.31% 30-year yield

The sequence explains why the criticism became so sharp.

| Date | Confirmed event | Policy and market meaning |
|---|---|---|
| August 5 | Treasury planned up to $38 billion of off-the-run liquidity-support buybacks and up to $25 billion of short-maturity cash-management buybacks for the quarter | Regular quarterly plan |
| August 17 | 30-year yield 5.31%, 10-year yield 4.72% | Long yields near a 19-year high |
| August 19 | Per-operation cap for 10-to-20-year and 20-to-30-year sectors raised from $2 billion to at least $4 billion | Additional announcement in the middle of the quarter |
| August 20 | 10-year yield 4.69%, 30-year yield 5.23% | Much of the first rally reversed |
| August 24 | Bessent said the enlarged operations begin September 9 and Treasury had not yet bought a single bond under the new terms | The effect so far was signaling, not execution |
| August 24-25 | Druckenmiller warned that the move was price management and risked Treasury credibility | The dispute became explicit |
| August 28 | 10-year yield 4.73%, 30-year yield 5.22% | The 30-year was 9bp below the peak; the 10-year was back near its pre-announcement level |

Treasury's [August 19 release](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0607) limited the change to two long-end nominal sectors. The increase runs from September 9 to the next quarterly refunding on November 4. Treasury said it had received consistently large volumes of high-quality offers in these sectors and could therefore provide more liquidity support.

That is a market-operations explanation. Bessent then said the program could be larger than $4 billion, that part of its purpose was signaling, and that yields did not reflect underlying fundamentals. The combination of his comments and the [market reaction reported by AP](https://apnews.com/article/rates-bond-market-bessent-inflation-c6e148f8235a98245adf04b2d4bdd8d1) makes it difficult to argue that Treasury was thinking only about trading mechanics.

Druckenmiller attacked that boundary. The core of his case, [reported by Reuters](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/us-treasury-buybacks-a-mistake-costing-credibility-says-druckenmiller-4874531), is that higher long yields should be treated as the bill for fiscal and inflation risk, not automatically as evidence of market failure.

## 2. A Treasury buyback does not make the debt disappear

The word buyback sounds as if the government is paying down debt. The current U.S. program usually does not do that.

Treasury buys older, less actively traded securities while continuing to issue new benchmark debt through regular auctions. The transaction removes harder-to-trade inventory from dealers and increases the relative share of more liquid securities. It changes the composition of debt available to the market more than the total stock of debt.

It is also different from Federal Reserve quantitative easing.

| Feature | Treasury buyback | Federal Reserve QE |
|---|---|---|
| Buyer | U.S. Treasury | Federal Reserve |
| Funding | Tax receipts, Treasury cash, or other debt issuance | Newly created central-bank reserves |
| Direct objective | Off-the-run liquidity, cash and maturity management | Easier financial conditions and monetary policy transmission |
| Effect on federal debt | Usually little change in the total | Treasury debt remains; the Fed balance sheet expands |
| Rate effect | Concentrated in targeted securities and maturity mix | Broad removal of duration risk from the market |

Calling the move money printing or QE is therefore mechanically wrong. There is still a duration effect. If Treasury finances purchases of long bonds with more bills, the public holds fewer long securities and more short securities. At sufficient scale, Treasury absorbs part of the market's duration risk. That can ease financial conditions at the margin without being QE.

## 3. Bessent's strongest argument is supported by evidence

The current buyback program did not begin under Bessent. It was launched in May 2024 to support off-the-run liquidity and improve cash management.

An [IMF empirical study published in 2025](https://www.imf.org/en/-/media/files/publications/wp/2025/english/wpiea2025088-print-pdf.pdf) found that buyback eligibility narrowed bid-ask spreads on targeted off-the-run securities by about 0.2 basis point and lifted prices by about 10 cents relative to comparable securities. Regular access to Treasury as a buyer lowers the inventory risk carried by primary dealers.

The effect was modest. Both estimates were less than one-tenth of a standard deviation, consistent with the small program size. But the tool worked in the direction intended.

Three parts of Bessent's defense are persuasive.

1. Illiquid long-dated off-the-run securities consume dealer balance-sheet capacity for longer. A regular buyer gives dealers an exit for that inventory.
2. Treasury is not canceling benchmark auctions. Bessent confirmed at his [August 24 press conference](https://transcripts.cnn.com/show/cnc/date/2026-08-24/segment/07) that the regular auction program would continue.
3. The enlarged buybacks have not begun. Until September 9, the observed yield response reflects an anticipated backstop, not actual Treasury demand.

If the Treasury market suddenly freezes, scaling this tool is sensible. A March 2020-style episode, in which dealers cannot absorb a wave of sales, would justify materially larger liquidity operations.

## 4. This episode looks more like a fiscal price than broken plumbing

Druckenmiller's objection is about the condition for using the tool. Long yields were high in mid-August, but trading had not stopped and Treasury auctions had not failed. A high yield is not, by itself, a liquidity crisis.

The fundamental forces pushing up yields were visible.

- The [Congressional Budget Office](https://www.cbo.gov/publication/61983) estimated a $1.8 trillion deficit for the first ten months of fiscal 2026 and raised its full-year estimate to $2.1 trillion.
- Federal debt crossed $40 trillion in August.
- Inflation remained above target while investors questioned the new Fed chair's reaction function.
- Heavy bond issuance by large technology companies financing AI data centers competed with Treasuries for investor capital.
- Treasury maintained regular coupon auction sizes and expected bill issuance to rise again with seasonal outflows.

Under these conditions, a 5%-plus 30-year yield can be compensation for bearing long-run fiscal and inflation risk. A durable reduction in yields requires a different deficit path and stronger inflation credibility, not only a tactical buyer.

Timing strengthens Druckenmiller's case. The [August 5 quarterly refunding plan](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0590) already set up to $38 billion of off-the-run liquidity buybacks for the quarter. Two weeks later, after the 30-year reached 5.31%, Treasury doubled the long-end per-operation cap outside the regular refunding announcement.

If a published liquidity metric had triggered the change, it would look like an operational adjustment. An off-cycle announcement after a yield spike, combined with explicit discussion of signaling, looks more like an implicit yield ceiling. Once investors believe a line exists, they have an incentive to test where it is. Each failed defense then requires a larger promise.

## 5. The market gave only a partial answer to both men

Long yields initially fell. The 30-year declined from 5.31% on August 17 to about 5.19% on the morning of August 26. Treasury-swap spreads also narrowed, suggesting that the burden of holding Treasuries had eased. [Axios concluded](https://www.axios.com/2026/08/26/treasury-bonds-borrowing-markets) that the signal was working in the short run.

The verdict did not last. After the Fed chair's Jackson Hole speech, [official Treasury data](https://home.treasury.gov/resource-center/data-chart-center/interest-rates/TextView?field_tdr_date_value=2026&type=daily_treasury_yield_curve) showed the 10-year at 4.73% and the 30-year at 5.22% on August 28. The 10-year was 1bp above its August 17 level; the 30-year remained 9bp below its peak.

The 10-year real yield stood at 2.42% and the 30-year real yield at 2.96% that day. The move cannot be explained by expected inflation alone. Investors still demanded substantial compensation for real duration, fiscal uncertainty, and debt supply.

The enlarged program has not yet executed a single operation. What has been tested is not the purchasing power of $4 billion but the signaling power of a promise to buy at least $4 billion. The signal can stabilize yields for days. Its reversal after one Fed speech shows that it is not a substitute for fundamentals.

| Question | Stronger case | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Can regular buybacks improve off-the-run liquidity? | Bessent | Empirical evidence shows a small but measurable benefit |
| Was the latest rise in long yields a market-function crisis? | Druckenmiller | Clear evidence of acute dysfunction was limited |
| Can $4 billion per operation change the direction of long yields for long? | Druckenmiller | It is small relative to a $2.1 trillion deficit and heavy issuance |
| Is the policy equivalent to QE? | Bessent | The buyer and funding mechanics are different |
| Can signaling reduce short-term volatility? | Bessent | The 30-year yield retreated from its peak |
| Should the program keep expanding without fiscal repair? | Druckenmiller | Credibility and escalation risks rise quickly |

Our judgment is straightforward: <strong>Bessent is right about the operating tool; Druckenmiller is more right about the signal sent in this episode</strong>. There is no reason to abolish buybacks. The danger begins when a high yield is labeled a market malfunction and Treasury becomes the judge of the correct long-term rate.

## 6. Four billion dollars is small, but the promise can become large

Relative to the Treasury market and quarterly issuance, $4 billion per operation is small. The behavioral effect is more important than the direct flow.

Investors now have to estimate how much more Treasury might buy whenever long yields rise. A perceived Treasury put makes short positions against long bonds less attractive. In the short run, that can reduce one-sided positioning and volatility.

The other side is moral hazard. If fiscal policy is protected from the market signal of higher borrowing costs, Congress has less incentive to reduce deficits. The next increase in yields may then require a larger buyback commitment.

A sustainable program needs three boundaries.

1. Purchases should remain concentrated in illiquid off-the-run securities.
2. Size should be tied to published metrics such as bid-ask spreads, dealer inventories, and auction tails.
3. Treasury should state clearly that it does not target a specific 10- or 30-year yield.

As of August 30, the official schedule linked by Treasury still shows the old $2 billion caps for post-September long-end operations. The August 19 release says the limits will be at least $4 billion and that an updated schedule will follow. The exact number of enlarged operations and the new quarterly total therefore remain unconfirmed.

## 7. The base case is temporary stabilization followed by another test

These probabilities and yield ranges are analytical scenarios, not official forecasts.

| Scenario | Probability | Policy and market path | Approximate 10-year and 30-year ranges | Equity implication |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| Rules-based liquidity support | 30% | Buybacks tied to transparent metrics and a regular schedule, alongside credible deficit consolidation | 10-year 4.4-4.7%, 30-year 4.9-5.2% | Stable discount rates support quality growth and Korean large caps |
| Treasury put without fiscal repair | 50% | Larger signals and operations appear when yields spike, but the deficit path does not improve | 10-year 4.6-5.1%, 30-year 5.1-5.6% | Indices hold up, but rotations between growth and value become frequent |
| Test of Treasury credibility | 20% | Inflation or auction shocks lead investors to test the implicit defense line and force escalation | 10-year above 5%, 30-year potentially above 5.6% | Long-duration and leveraged equities de-rate; the dollar strengthens |

The first path requires the November 4 quarterly refunding to pair objective buyback rules with a credible fiscal plan. The second is the current base case. The policy limits volatility, but the fiscal deficit and real yields keep the floor for long-term borrowing costs high.

The third path is more likely to appear first in auctions than in the buyback operation itself. Large auction tails and a falling indirect-bidder share in 20- and 30-year sales would show that private demand for duration is weakening.

## 8. For Korean equities, the direction of credibility matters more than the headline

U.S. long yields affect Korean equities even when they do not immediately change Korean corporate cash flows. They move the global discount rate, the dollar, foreign portfolio flows, and the cost of dollar funding together.

### AI Big Tech and semiconductors face opposite earnings and valuation forces

Stable long yields lower the cost of financing data-center expansion for Microsoft, Amazon, Alphabet, and Meta. They also help AI cloud companies dependent on bonds and supplier financing. That supports the duration of demand for GPUs, HBM, and server DRAM.

If the 10-year real yield remains near or above 2.5%, the present value of distant cash flows falls. Even if Big Tech continues to spend, investors will demand a clearer path to free-cash-flow recovery. Samsung Electronics and SK hynix can deliver higher earnings while receiving a lower valuation multiple.

Memory investors should separate two variables. HBM contract prices and shipments determine earnings. The U.S. 10-year real yield helps determine the multiple attached to those earnings. When strong results and weak share prices coexist, the first task is to identify which variable changed.

### Banks and insurers are not automatic winners from higher long yields

A steeper curve can help bank lending spreads and insurers' reinvestment yields. It can also produce bond valuation losses, higher deposit and wholesale funding costs, and worse credit quality. An orderly rise in yields is different from a jump caused by damaged fiscal credibility.

### The won and foreign flows react first to risk appetite

If buybacks suppress rate volatility and restrain dollar strength, foreign flows into Korean equities improve. If the credibility dispute pushes real yields and the dollar higher together, foreign selling can appear even while Korean earnings estimates remain strong.

## 9. September 9 and November 4 provide measurable tests

The debate can be judged with data rather than rhetoric.

1. After September 9, track the amount offered, the amount accepted, and the number of CUSIPs purchased in long-end operations. Large offers with selective acceptance would support a liquidity-management interpretation.
2. Track off-the-run versus on-the-run spreads and bid-ask spreads. Improvement would validate Bessent's operational argument.
3. Track 10- and 30-year real yields. Lower nominal yields alongside higher inflation expectations would not be high-quality stabilization.
4. Track auction tails and the indirect-bidder share in 20- and 30-year auctions. Private demand must recover if reliance on Treasury buybacks is to remain limited.
5. At the November 4 refunding, look for the long-end buyback cap, the bill share of issuance, and a fiscal-consolidation plan together.

Druckenmiller's warning gains support if auction demand remains weak and real yields continue to rise after the buybacks begin. Bessent's defense gains support if small purchases improve off-the-run liquidity, benchmark auctions remain solid, and volatility falls without repeated expansion.

## Conclusion: fixing the market is different from changing its answer

Treasury buybacks are a useful operating tool. A regular and predictable program that helps investors trade older securities has a legitimate role, and the empirical evidence shows a modest liquidity benefit.

This dispute concerns why and when the tool was enlarged. Treasury doubled the cap outside the regular refunding process after long yields reached a 19-year high, and Bessent did not hide the signaling objective or his disagreement with the market's pricing. On that point, Druckenmiller's criticism is more accurate.

Four billion dollars is small relative to the Treasury market. It becomes more consequential if investors interpret it as a promise that the government will not accept an uncomfortable long-term yield. Repairing market plumbing and changing the fiscal message carried by market prices are different policies.

Korean investors should therefore monitor more than the buyback headline. The U.S. 10-year real yield, demand at 30-year auctions, the actual allocation of long-end buybacks, and the November fiscal plan will decide whether strong semiconductor earnings translate into share prices or continue to collide with lower valuation multiples.
