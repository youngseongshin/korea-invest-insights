At its Investor Day on October 28, Oracle set a date for the end of its cash burn. It expects **positive free cash flow in the second half of fiscal 2028**, the six months from December 2027 to May 2028, and **at least $30 billion of free cash flow in fiscal 2029**. It capped new capital-market funding through fiscal 2030 at **$20 billion**, currently expected to be $10 billion of equity and $10 billion of gross debt. [Oracle Investor Day release](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1341439/000119312526435853/d75538dex991.htm)

Before the event, analysts expected free cash flow of about **minus $35 billion in both fiscal 2027 and fiscal 2028**, according to FactSet figures cited by CNBC. [CNBC, Oct 28](https://www.cnbc.com/2026/10/28/oracle-forecast-growth-2030.html) The stock rose **10.7% to $144.54**. It is still about 49% below its 52-week high of $281.62.

The headline revenue target was not the news. Oracle's fiscal 2030 goal of at least $225 billion matched the LSEG consensus of $224.2 billion. The news was the cash.

**Our judgment: the cash turn is realistic on Oracle's own definition, and it came early because Oracle stopped paying first.** The upfront cost is now carried by customer prepayments, customer-supplied hardware and landlord-built data centers, and Oracle expects supplier credit to add to them. That moves most of the upfront cost off Oracle's cash-flow statement. It does not remove it. The variable that decides whether the plan holds is **whether Oracle's largest AI customers, above all OpenAI, keep paying ahead of delivery**.

This article is dated October 29, 2026. Oracle's fiscal year ends on May 31. Prices are NYSE closes on October 28.

## Five things to take away

- **Half of Oracle's operating cash last quarter came from customer prepayments.** Operating cash flow was $23.1 billion in the quarter to August 31. Of that, $11.4 billion was prepayment. Without it, free cash flow would have been about minus $16.8 billion instead of minus $5.4 billion.
- **Oracle has signed $288 billion of data-center leases that have not started yet.** That is 2.3 times its $125 billion of borrowings. These leases begin between late 2026 and fiscal 2029 and run 15 to 19 years. Their upfront building cost does not appear in Oracle's capex line.
- **The timeline matches S&P's test.** S&P cut Oracle to BBB- in July and, according to press reports, said the rating could come under pressure if free cash flow is not positive in 2028. Five of the six months in Oracle's target window fall in calendar 2028.
- **Oracle measures leverage honestly, and that target matters more than free cash flow.** Its goal of net debt below 3.5 times adjusted EBITDA in fiscal 2029 counts leases and vendor financing as debt. On our rough estimate, it needs adjusted EBITDA of about $85–100 billion that year.
- **The stock trades at about 7 times Oracle's fiscal 2030 EPS target of $21.** The market believes the cash plan more than it did on Tuesday. It still does not believe the earnings plan.

The release states the method in one sentence. Vendor financing, customer-provided hardware and customer prepayments are expected to reduce upfront cash needs until free cash flow turns positive. Leases appear in the leverage footnote.

## Why the turn came early: Oracle stopped paying first

### Customers prepay

In the quarter to August 31, Oracle's operating cash flow included **$11.4 billion of customer prepayments** with a significant financing component. In all of fiscal 2026, such prepayments were $4.6 billion. In the two years before, they were zero. [Oracle 10-Q, Q1 FY27](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1341439/000119312526389274/orcl-20260831.htm)

![Oracle's cash bridge for the quarter from June to August 2026. Operating cash excluding prepayments was $11.7 billion. Customer prepayments added $11.4 billion, for operating cash flow of $23.1 billion. Capital expenditures were $28.5 billion, leaving free cash flow of minus $5.4 billion.](/post-assets/oracle-fcf-turn-customers-landlords-pay-first-openai-test-2026-10-29/q1-cash-bridge.en.png)

A prepayment is cash today for service delivered later. It improves free cash flow now and lowers it later, when Oracle recognizes the revenue without collecting new cash. Because the contracts carry a financing component, Oracle also records interest on that money, an immaterial amount so far. In economic terms, a large prepayment is a loan from the customer.

On the September call, according to a third-party transcript, the CFO said net cash capex in fiscal 2027 would be no more than $70 billion after prepayments, against gross capex of $90–95 billion. If the whole gap is prepayments, that implies **roughly $20–25 billion of prepayments this fiscal year**. That figure is our inference.

### Customers bring their own hardware

Some customers now supply the accelerators themselves. Co-CEO Clay Magouyrk described this on the September call as a "bring your own hardware" model, according to the same transcript. Oracle then provides power, space, networking and operations. It earns less revenue per gigawatt, but it also spends far less capex. A Wells Fargo note cited by TipRanks estimated about $5.9 billion of revenue per gigawatt for contracts where customers bring their own chips or pay upfront, against about $17.3 billion for standard ones. [TipRanks, Oct 2026](https://www.tipranks.com/news/article/oracles-investor-day-is-coming-heres-the-answer-wells-fargo-wants-on-the-stock)

This creates a tension inside the plan. Every contract that moves to customer hardware helps the cash target and makes the $225 billion revenue target harder.

### Suppliers wait to be paid

Vendor financing means equipment suppliers or their financiers accept later payment. Oracle expects it to help, but the latest quarter does not show it yet. Unpaid capex rose to $6.2 billion at August 31, from $4.0 billion a year earlier. However, Oracle's cash-flow line for short-term financing related to capex was a **net repayment of $0.8 billion** in the quarter, against an inflow of $2.0 billion a year earlier. Oracle counts vendor financing in its net debt definition. That is the right treatment.

### Landlords build the data centers

This is the largest mechanism and the least discussed. At August 31, Oracle had **$288 billion of lease commitments that had not yet commenced**, substantially all for data centers. They are expected to start between the second quarter of fiscal 2027 and fiscal 2029, for terms of 15 to 19 years. The figure was $260 billion at May 31. [Oracle 10-Q, Q1 FY27](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1341439/000119312526389274/orcl-20260831.htm)

![Oracle obligations at August 31, 2026: total borrowings of $125 billion, lease liabilities on the balance sheet of $43.8 billion, leases signed but not yet commenced of $288 billion, and unconditional purchase obligations of $34.2 billion.](/post-assets/oracle-fcf-turn-customers-landlords-pay-first-openai-test-2026-10-29/obligations.en.png)

When Oracle leases a building, the developer pays for construction. Oracle pays rent later. The construction cost does not appear in Oracle's capex line, so it does not reduce free cash flow as capex. Oracle has occasionally supported a lessor directly: its 10-K disclosed a guarantee of up to $3.3 billion of one lessor's borrowing, which matured in September 2026. Operating-lease rent does reduce operating cash flow once the lease starts, and finance-lease principal is recorded as a financing outflow.

A rough scale helps. If the $288 billion is paid evenly over 15 to 19 years, rent on these leases alone would be about **$15–19 billion a year** once all of them have started. In the quarter to August, Oracle paid about $1.1 billion of lease cash in total. That straight-line split is our estimate, not Oracle's disclosure.

### Why Oracle gave a date now

In September, Oracle declined to say when free cash flow would turn positive. About seven weeks later it gave a date. Credit and equity pressure explain the timing.

- **The credit test.** S&P cut Oracle to BBB- on July 9, one notch above speculative grade, and cited concentration on OpenAI, according to [MLQ](https://mlq.ai/news/sp-downgrades-oracle-to-bbb-one-notch-above-junk-citing-openai-concentration-risk/). According to Fortune, S&P said the rating could come under pressure if free cash flow is not positive in 2028. [Fortune, Sep 18](https://fortune.com/2026/09/18/sp-kept-oracle-investment-grade-its-own-numbers-dont-support-that-call/) Five of the six months in Oracle's target window, December 2027 to May 2028, fall in that year.
- **The equity test.** The stock had fallen by about half from its 52-week high. Investors feared repeated share sales. The $20 billion cap, with only $10 billion of equity, answers that fear. At the October 28 market value of about $395 billion, $10 billion is about 2.5% of the company.

## How Oracle defines free cash flow

Oracle defines free cash flow as operating cash flow minus capital expenditures. Under that definition:

| Item | Where it goes | Effect on free cash flow |
|---|---|---|
| Customer prepayments | Operating cash flow | Raises it now, lowers it later |
| Customer-supplied hardware | Not on Oracle's books | Lowers capex and revenue |
| Vendor financing | Financing activities (short-term financing related to capex) | Eases cash needs; counted as debt in Oracle's leverage target |
| Building cost of leased data centers | Not capex | Never counted as capex |
| Operating-lease rent | Operating cash flow | Lowers it from lease start |
| Finance-lease principal | Financing cash flow | Not counted |

Sources: Oracle 10-K for fiscal 2026 and 10-Q for the quarter to August 31; our classification of where each item lands.

None of this breaks an accounting rule. It does mean free cash flow now measures a smaller share of what the AI build-out costs. Oracle's leverage target corrects for that. Its net debt includes lease liabilities and vendor financing, and its adjusted EBITDA adds back operating-lease expense. **Investors should treat the 3.5x leverage target as the main promise and the free-cash-flow date as the secondary one.**

## Is it realistic?

**Fiscal 2027.** Annualizing last quarter's $11.7 billion of operating cash before prepayments gives about $47 billion. Subtracting net cash capex of $70 billion gives free cash flow of about **minus $20–25 billion**. That is better than the minus $35 billion consensus, and it depends on $20–25 billion of prepayments actually arriving.

**The turn in fiscal 2028.** Positive free cash flow in the second half requires capex to fall from its peak while new capacity starts earning. Oracle said capex peaks in fiscal 2027–2028. The second-half target is plausible if Oracle delivers capacity on schedule.

**Fiscal 2029.** At least $30 billion of free cash flow needs both lower capex and much higher revenue. The backlog helps. Oracle's remaining performance obligations were **$664 billion** at August 31. Oracle expects to recognize about 13% in the next 12 months, 37% in months 13 to 36, and 34% in months 37 to 60. [Oracle 10-Q](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/1341439/000119312526389274/orcl-20260831.htm) That schedule implies roughly $120 billion a year of backlog revenue in months 13 to 36 (September 2027 to August 2029) and about $110 billion a year in months 37 to 60. On our estimate, fiscal 2030's $225 billion target therefore needs roughly half its revenue from contracts not yet signed, renewals and usage.

**Leverage in fiscal 2029.** If all the signed leases commence, lease liabilities could rise by roughly $170–190 billion in present-value terms, using the 5.7% average discount rate on Oracle's existing leases. Add the existing $43.8 billion of lease liabilities and borrowings of about $135 billion after the new debt, subtract cash, and net debt on Oracle's definition could reach about **$300–350 billion**. To stay below 3.5x, adjusted EBITDA would need to be about **$85–100 billion**. That is our estimate, not Oracle's, and it is achievable only if revenue arrives on the backlog schedule.

**The sensitivity.** Oracle gave no fiscal 2029 revenue figure. Our assumption is about $170 billion on the path to $225 billion, so a 10% shortfall is roughly $17 billion. At an assumed incremental EBITDA margin of about 55%, it would remove about $9 billion of cash, close to a third of the $30 billion target. Rent and interest do not shrink when revenue is late.

## The customer at the center: OpenAI

Oracle's 10-K says no customer accounted for 10% of revenue in fiscal 2026. Backlog is different. ERP Today reports that roughly half of Oracle's $664 billion backlog is tied to OpenAI. Oracle does not disclose the share. [ERP Today, Sep 2026](https://erp.today/oracle-q1-fy27-results-664b-backlog-ai-contracts/)

That concentration links these mechanisms. A prepayment needs a customer with cash. Customer-supplied hardware needs a customer that can buy chips. Vendors and landlords plausibly lend and build because they see contracts with creditworthy end customers. If OpenAI's fundraising slows, every one of these sources weakens at once, and the leases keep running for 15 to 19 years.

The bull case on this point is real. ERP Today reported that Oracle's non-OpenAI backlog more than doubled in a year. On the call, the CFO said Oracle booked more than $30 billion of new AI contracts last quarter, and most of them used prepayment or customer hardware. [ERP Today, Sep 2026](https://erp.today/oracle-q1-fy27-results-664b-backlog-ai-contracts/) Each new prepaying customer reduces the OpenAI dependence. That diversification is the number to watch.

## Our judgment, and what would change it

The free-cash-flow date is achievable. Oracle has changed its contracts so that customers, suppliers and landlords carry the upfront cost. **Customer funding is the variable that dominates**, ahead of capex timing and margins. If the largest customers keep prepaying, Oracle reaches positive free cash flow in fiscal 2028 and the leverage target in fiscal 2029. If they stop, Oracle's own definition will show it within one or two quarters, because the prepayment line will shrink while rent starts.

| Evidence | What it would mean |
|---|---|
| Prepayments of about $3–4.5bn a quarter for the rest of the year | The pace needed for $20–25bn; the fiscal 2027 cash plan is on track |
| Non-OpenAI backlog keeps growing faster than total backlog | Concentration falls; the credit case improves |
| S&P moves the outlook to positive or affirms after the Investor Day | The 2028 test is accepted as credible |
| Prepayments fall sharply while lease commencements continue | Cash support is fading while fixed costs rise |
| Oracle raises fiscal 2028 capex above the fiscal 2027 level | The step-down is slipping, and so is the second-half turn |
| OpenAI delays payments or cuts capacity requests | The plan's central assumption fails |

The next check is Oracle's fiscal second-quarter report in December. The prepayment line in operating cash flow and the change in leases not yet commenced will matter more than revenue.
