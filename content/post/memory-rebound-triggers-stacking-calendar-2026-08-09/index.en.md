This piece takes up the next question. So what ends this phase? It scores the candidates across five axes for impact and timing, then lays out why no single one is enough and what to watch first.

## TL;DR

- Start with the record of last week's three days. On August 5 the KOSPI rose 3.76% (SK Hynix up 5.77%); on the 6th it fell 4.58% (SK Hynix down 10.37%); on the 7th it fell another 0.60%. [Fact: exchange, press] <strong>The trigger for the plunge on the 6th was not macro but guidance from AMD and SanDisk</strong>. Oil prices and interest rates were actually favorable that day.
- These three days are the starting point for this piece. Even when macro lines up favorably, a single semiconductor-specific catalyst can still take a stock down more than 10% in a day. <strong>Macro is a condition that supports the floor, not one that resolves the narrative</strong>.
- The macro axis has already fired once. On August 7, the US July jobs report came in well short of expectations, and the probability of a September rate hike collapsed while the probability of a hold flipped to about 60%. [Fact: press] Even so, the KOSPI still closed lower that day.
- The nearest major event is NVIDIA's earnings on August 26, but <strong>this will be the fourth straight quarter in which a beat has failed to lift the stock</strong>. NVIDIA beat estimates in August and November 2025 and February and May 2026, yet the reaction was flat or negative each time, and the 52-week high came before the May report, not after it. [Fact: market data] The beat is already priced in.
- So what matters on August 26 is not this quarter's numbers but next quarter's guidance and customer mix. On the disclosed figures, <strong>top-customer concentration is actually declining</strong>. Four customers accounting for 63% of revenue in fiscal Q3 2026 became three customers accounting for 54% in fiscal Q1 2027. [Fact: compiled from quarterly filings]
- Shareholder returns carry the greatest impact, but this week a condition got attached to that axis. <strong>On August 7, SK Hynix announced a new fab investment of roughly $38bn</strong>. [Fact: press] When a returns announcement arrives alongside a capacity expansion, its power to lift the valuation floor weakens. The two decisions are two sides of the same capital allocation choice.
- Export data has little impact but the highest frequency. The Korea Customs Service publishes figures three times a month (the 11th, the 21st, and the 1st of the following month), which keeps score on the contract-price debate on an ongoing basis. July semiconductor exports came to $41.1bn, up 178.8% year on year but down 8.5% from the prior month. [Fact: Korea Customs Service]
- The conclusion is stacking. On July 30, Samsung Electronics reported its highest-ever earnings and closed lower anyway. This is a phase in which single events get sold into, and what is needed is <strong>a three-layer structure in which returns build on top of a macro landing and an NVIDIA confirmation</strong>. That sequence is already laid out on the calendar from late August through year-end.
- The earliest signal is not on the trigger list. Since July, good news has lasted one day and bad news three. The moment <strong>the market posts its first two consecutive up days on good news</strong> is the empirical marker that the reaction function has changed, and the two days following NVIDIA's earnings are the observation window for it.

<div class="thesis-callout__label">Key Framing</div>

Right now the market prices in good news for one day and bad news for three. As long as this asymmetry holds, no single trigger can build a trend. NVIDIA beating estimates for four straight quarters without its stock rising, and Samsung Electronics closing lower on the day it posted record earnings, are the same phenomenon. What investors should count, then, is not the number of triggers but the order in which they arrive, and what to check first is not the news itself but whether the reaction to the news has changed.

The trigger identified for the plunge on the 6th was that AMD and SanDisk guidance had missed market expectations, dragging down the US semiconductor index the night before. [Fact: press reports] There is a point worth noting here. <strong>Macro was not bad that day</strong>. These three days set the premise for this piece. Before listing rebound candidates, the question to ask is not what news is coming but how the market is responding to news.

The August 11 preliminary export figures have a fixed date, but that alone will not change the trend, while confirmation of Samsung's HBM4 customer qualification would trigger the biggest re-rating, but there is no way to know when it will come.

## 3. Macro Has Already Fired Once

Macro easing, long flagged as a catalyst still to come, partly materialized last Friday. The US July jobs report, released on August 7, came in well short of expectations, and the probability of a September rate hike collapsed while the probability of a hold flipped to about 60%. [Fact: press] That is a reversal from as recently as late July, when the probability of a hike had stood above 80%.

Yet <strong>the KOSPI still closed lower that day</strong>. News that eased the discount-rate burden came out, and Korean semiconductor stocks did not respond to it. It is the same structure as August 6. Semiconductors do not rise even when macro is good, and they still fall on a good macro day if a semiconductor-specific negative shows up.

This week's calendar is packed with macro data. US CPI comes on August 12, PPI on the 13th, and retail sales on the 14th, and Treasury auctions land on the same dates: $42bn of 10-year notes on the 12th and $25bn of 30-year bonds on the 13th. [Fact: Treasury Department] Because inflation data and long-bond auctions fall on the same days, rate volatility could pick up, and that cuts both ways.

Macro is a necessary condition. If it breaks down, a rebound is difficult even if every other axis is favorable, and if it improves on its own, that alone will not bring a rebound either.

## 5. Shareholder Returns: High Impact, Now With a Condition Attached

Shareholder returns carry the greatest impact because they raise the valuation floor by contract. As discussed in an earlier piece, the market is currently pricing memory earnings with a low persistence rate, but a returns policy fixes the shareholder's share regardless of that persistence rate. The greater the doubt about how long profits will last, the greater the relative value of a committed return.

Here is where the two companies stand. Samsung Electronics has a policy, running from 2024 through 2026, of returning 50% of free cash flow and paying a regular annual dividend of 9.8tn won, and it has disclosed a condition that it will consider additional returns if cumulative three-year return capacity exceeds the existing dividend. Net cash stood at 167.6tn won at the end of the second quarter. On July 31, the company said it was discussing a concrete plan that would include a special dividend. SK Hynix has a policy, running from 2025 through 2027, of a fixed dividend of 1,500 won per share plus roughly 50% of cumulative free cash flow as the source of returns, and at its July 29 earnings call it said additional returns were under review and would be announced within the year. On August 7, when it set a quarterly dividend of 375 won, it reconfirmed that it would finalize an additional returns plan during the third quarter. Net cash stood at 69.4tn won at the end of the second quarter. [Fact: both companies' disclosures and earnings calls]

<strong>Yet on that same August 7, SK Hynix also announced a new fab investment of roughly $38bn</strong>. That covers 35.2tn won for DRAM at the Yongin P2 fab and 19.1tn won for NAND at the Cheongju M17 fab, both running through 2031. [Fact: press]

The fact that these two announcements landed on the same day changes the character of the shareholder-returns trigger. A large part of the discount the market applies to memory stocks reflects concern about 2028 supply, and when a returns announcement arrives alongside a capacity-expansion announcement, <strong>it becomes unclear whether capital allocation is leaning toward discipline or toward expansion</strong>. Returns do the most to lift the valuation floor when they read as a signal of supply discipline. Raising dividends and capacity at the same time lets the two messages cancel each other out.

So the amount is not the only thing to check in a returns announcement. It also needs to spell out whether the company will carry out a share buyback and cancellation, how long the policy period runs, and how it relates to capital expenditure plans over the same period. For reference, the 100tn won figure circulating in the market has not been confirmed by the company. [Blocked: unconfirmed by the company]

Here is where July stood. Total exports came to $98.89bn, up 62.8% year on year and the highest ever for a July, and semiconductor exports came to $41.1bn, up 178.8% and the second-highest on record. On a month-on-month basis, though, they fell 8.5%, because June had been the all-time high at $44.8bn. [Fact: Korea Customs Service, Ministry of Trade, Industry and Energy]

Any single release has little impact on its own. Exports are a lagging indicator confirming transactions that have already happened, so they cannot resolve the 2028 profitability debate. Instead, this data's value lies in its frequency. It is the only public source that measures, three times a month, where contract prices are actually heading, and dividing export value by volume gives an approximation of unit-price direction. What to watch in the August data is not the year-on-year growth rate but whether the month-on-month decline seen in July gets reversed.

## 7. Stacking and the Calendar

Putting the scoring together, the conclusion is that a single trigger is not enough. The evidence is what actually happened in July. On July 30, Samsung Electronics reported record quarterly operating profit of 89.5tn won, and its stock rose more than 4% intraday before reversing to close lower. NVIDIA beat estimates for four straight quarters without its stock rising. <strong>One good number does not end this phase</strong>.

NVIDIA's 2023 episode is often cited, but even that case was not simple. After its May 2023 report, when it guided next-quarter revenue to 1.5 times consensus, the stock jumped around 25% in a single day, but it then traded sideways through the summer and fall, and when it beat by a wide margin again in November, the stock actually fell more than 2% the next day. The trend was only confirmed after that. [Fact: market data] It took a chain of confirmations, not one.

The calendar lays out the catalysts in sequence: preliminary export figures on August 11; US inflation data, retail sales, and Treasury auctions from the 12th through the 14th; NVIDIA earnings on the 26th; Jackson Hole from the 27th through the 29th; confirmed August export figures on September 1; the FOMC meeting on September 15 and 16; Micron earnings at the end of September; and then SK Hynix's returns plan sometime in the third quarter along with third-quarter earnings at the end of October.

This sequence divides into three layers by function. A macro landing builds the floor. On top of that, NVIDIA earnings, export data, and product qualification confirm demand. Finally, shareholder returns and third-quarter earnings move the valuation. If only the first two layers fill in, the result is a technical rebound at best; a trend reversal requires all three.

## 8. The Earliest Signal Is Not a Trigger

One last signal, not on the list above. Since July, this market's defining trait has been that good news doesn't last a day while bad news lasts three. The record surge on July 31 was followed by the plunge on August 3, and the rise on August 5 was followed by the plunge on the 6th.

<strong>The moment the market posts its first two consecutive up days on good news is the empirical evidence that the reaction function has changed</strong>. This is likely to show up before any of the triggers do, and it is simple to check: watch the cumulative reaction over the two days following NVIDIA's August 26 earnings. If the stock rises on day one and again on day two, the market has started digesting good news; if it rises on day one and gives that back on day two, it is still the same phase.

Here are the conditions that would overturn this piece's verdict. If Samsung's HBM4 customer qualification is confirmed, if third-quarter server DRAM contract prices come in well above forecasts, or if shareholder returns are announced alongside a scaled-back capacity plan, the impact ranking above would shift. Conversely, if August exports fall again month on month and NVIDIA guides next-quarter revenue below consensus, the three-layer structure this piece lays out would stall at the first stage.
