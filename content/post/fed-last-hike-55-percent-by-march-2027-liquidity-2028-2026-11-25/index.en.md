The Fed cut rates three times in the second half of 2025, then hiked again this September after the Iran war sent oil higher from late February. The policy rate is now 3.75% to 4.00%, and futures have almost fully priced another hike on December 9.

That leaves markets with two questions. When do the hikes stop, and when does money get easier again? We rebuilt the 2027 path from 64 series in the Federal Reserve Economic Data (FRED) database and the Treasury's daily statements, then checked it against 23 outlook reports for 2027 from Korean brokerages.

<strong>Our view: there is a 55% chance the Fed's hikes end by March 2027. The paths where the peak reaches 4.75% or higher still add up to 45%, so we see no good reason to bet on a lower peak than futures already price. The liquidity that lifts asset prices comes with rate cuts, and those centre on the first half of 2028. Every probability in this piece is our own subjective estimate.</strong>

- Headline inflation falls in spring. Base effects already set by 2026 oil prices take CPI down to 2.4% to 2.7% in May 2027, then back up to 3.3% to 3.6% in July.
- The core inflation the Fed watches does not fall on its own. Core PCE stays at 3.0% at the end of 2027 even if it rises only 0.25% a month. Reaching the Fed's 2.5% projection needs 0.21% a month.
- Japan and China sold Treasuries, but by roughly half to two-thirds of the reported drop. The problem is who holds the debt. Price-sensitive holders now own 70% of coupon securities.
- Debt-limit politics set the 2027 liquidity path. Settlement liquidity such as bank reserves improves by the end of 2027 with 75% probability, while the chance of a rate cut in 2027 is 25%.
- Next checks: the December 9 FOMC and dot plot, the December 11 funding deadline, and core inflation prints in January and February.

## The Fed is at 3.75% to 4.00% and wants one more hike

In the October FOMC minutes almost all participants judged further tightening appropriate. A couple said they could consider a move larger than 25bp if the data were poor, and Governor Waller publicly raised a 50bp hike for December.

The table shows where 2027 starts. The two rows to watch are core PCE and reserves.

| Indicator | Now | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Policy rate | 3.75% to 4.00% | 4.00% to 4.25% after a December hike |
| 2-year / 10-year Treasury | 4.63% / 4.92% (Nov 23) | 4.88% / 5.15% on Nov 13. The 10-year peaked at 5.31% on Oct 5 |
| Headline / core CPI | about 3.95% / about 2.77% (Oct) | Our estimate; October 2025 is interpolated |

Core PCE has accelerated for three months. Reserves, the money banks keep at the Fed, are back to their 2025 low as a share of GDP (9.05%). The year begins with both rates and liquidity tight.

## The last hike is most likely in the first quarter

Three pieces of evidence point to the peak, and each has a limit.

The first is market pricing. Fed funds futures (November 23 settlements) imply 4.14% for December, 4.44% for March and 4.54% for June. That is 2.6 hikes from here. The figure is an average across many paths, so it does not say June is the last hike. November 23 also followed a sharp equity sell-off, which pulled the reading down.

The second is the Fed. The September dot plot has a median of 4.1% for the end of both 2026 and 2027. It is a year-end number, so it includes paths that hike further and then cut, and it says nothing about when the last hike comes. The most common 2027 dot is 4.375%, from 8 of 18 participants.

The third is forecasters. Ten Korean brokerages split evenly: five see the hikes ending in December at 4.25%, five see one more in the first quarter to 4.50%. Most of the December calls were written before the October CPI and the Waller remarks, and the ten share the same data, so they are not ten independent samples. Global houses, according to press reports, range from a December stop (Citi, Fitch) to hikes in December, January and March (JPMorgan, Deutsche Bank).

One argument says the Fed will struggle to keep hiking while headline inflation is falling in spring. We give it little weight. The Fed looks at how broad core inflation is, not at base effects it can forecast. The minutes say most participants described inflation pressures as broad based, and the fall in headline is already in the staff forecast.

So the peak depends on when core inflation cools. Three things can stop the hikes: core running at 0.25% a month or less, tighter financial conditions doing the Fed's work through lower stocks and wider credit spreads, or an accident in credit or funding markets.

| Scenario | Last hike | Peak (upper bound) | Probability |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | December 2026 | 4.25% | 20% |
| B | January to March 2027 | 4.50% | 35% |
| C | April to September 2027 | 4.75% | 28% |
| D | Later, or no peak in 2027 | 5.00% or higher | 17% |

That gives 55% for an end by March and 83% for an end by September. Weighting the four scenarios gives about 2.5 hikes, almost the same as the 2.6 in futures. We could not find grounds to bet on a lower peak than the market.

### Markets become confident of the peak around the June 9 meeting

If core inflation cools from December to February, confidence forms between mid-April, when March CPI is released, and the June 9 FOMC with its new dot plot. If it does not, the calendar works against a clean read. May CPI, the low point for headline, arrives after the June meeting, and headline rises again in July. Confidence then slips to the September 15 meeting. The centre is June; the range runs from mid-April to September.

We put 50% on the October 5 high of 5.31% being the cycle peak for the 10-year yield. Using realised volatility over the past year (72bp annualised), the chance of touching 5.31% again before the end of 2027 is 42% to 60%.

For the first cut we use 25% in 2027, 35% in the first half of 2028 and 40% later. In six past cycles (1995, 1997, 2000, 2006, 2018, 2023) the gap from the last hike to the first cut ran from 5.1 to 18.2 months, with a median of 10.7. The sample is small and all six were demand-led, so it fits a supply shock poorly. There is no FOMC meeting in December 2027.

## Headline inflation falls in spring and rises again in summer

Physical Brent in 2026 averaged $103 to $117 from March to May, $84 to $91 from June to August, and $114 to $124 in September and October. Year-on-year inflation in 2027 traces that shape in reverse.

What changes is when headline dips below 3% and where it ends the year. In the base case the dip below 3% is brief, in April and May, and headline is back above 3% by year-end.

## The core inflation the Fed watches does not cool on its own

Core PCE, the Fed's target variable, was 3.12% in September. Even if it rises 0.25% every month from here, it ends 2027 at 3.04%, barely changed. Reaching the Fed's September projection of 2.5% for 2027 needs 0.21% a month. The last three months averaged 0.24%, and September was 0.32%.

![Core PCE, year on year, actual and projected to the end of 2027 under four monthly paces. At 0.30% a month it reaches about 3.7%, at 0.25% it stays at 3.0%, at 0.21% it meets the Fed's 2.5% projection, and at 0.18% it falls to 2.2%.](/post-assets/fed-last-hike-55-percent-by-march-2027-liquidity-2028-2026-11-25/core-pce-paths.en.png)

Forces push core in both directions.

- Pushing down: the tariff effect is fading. Core goods CPI fell from 1.2% in March to 0.8% in October, and the minutes say tariff effects appear to be waning. Wage growth is 3.1%, unit labour costs 2.4% and productivity growth 2.1%.
- Holding up: the gap between diesel and crude prices is 1.8 times its 2025 average. The US Energy Information Administration (EIA) expects refined-product supply to stay constrained to the end of 2027. Shelter inflation is 3.4% and households' long-run inflation expectations are 3.3%.

A lower headline rate is not enough for a peak. The peak needs evidence that core has stopped rising, and a cut needs core at about 0.21% a month. We put 48% on core PCE running at 2.75% or less on a three-month annualised basis, with headline CPI below 3%, in the data through June 2027.

## AI investment is now an inflation variable

Can AI data centres raise inflation through commodity prices? On demand share alone the effect looks small. The Fed's own assessment and the estimates say otherwise.

According to the minutes, many participants viewed the AI buildout as a material source of inflation risk, and said price pressures were spreading beyond industries directly tied to data-centre construction. Hanwha Investment & Securities estimates that AI adds 0.29 percentage points to core PCE at the end of 2026. That is half the gap of about 0.6 points between core PCE today and the Fed's projection.

Commodity prices show the same pressure. Copper averaged $14,473 a tonne in September, 45% higher than a year earlier. Aluminium is up 24% and tin 56%. AI and data centres take only 3.5% to 4.4% of copper demand, but in a market where supply responds slowly a small rise in demand moves prices a lot.

It is hard to argue the pressure fades in 2027. Hyperscaler capital spending is expected to rise from $735bn to $805bn in 2026 to $1.07tn to $1.1tn in 2027, an increase of 33% to 50%. Growth slows, but the dollar increase is more than $250bn.

There is a risk in the other direction. About 46% of this spending is externally financed, and most hyperscalers run negative free cash flow in 2027. If funding dries up, investment falls and the price pressure goes with it. That path would arrive as an accident, not as a gentle slowdown.

## A new closure of Hormuz would erase the spring decline

The EIA, the International Energy Agency (IEA) and the large investment banks expect a 2027 oil surplus of 2 to 3.8 million barrels a day and Brent at $75 to $78 in the fourth quarter. Those forecasts assume normal traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, which has not been confirmed. The US Strategic Petroleum Reserve has fallen from 415 million barrels in February to 276 million, so the buffer is thinner.

We put 65% on headline CPI dipping below 3% at least once in April or May 2027, and 40% on it being at or below 3% at year-end. Another oil spike would lower both.

## Japan and China sold, but by less than the reported numbers

Worries about Treasury demand start with Japan and China. At the end of September Japan held $1,063bn, down $126bn in a year, and China held $564bn, down $135bn and the lowest since 2008.

These figures are at market value. Yields on 2-year to 10-year notes rose 1.1 to 1.35 percentage points over the same period, which cuts the value of an unchanged portfolio by 5% to 7%. Taking that out gives the following (in $bn, our estimates).

| Holder | Reported change | Net flow after valuation effect |
|---|---|---|
| Japan | -126 | -40 to -66 |
| China | -135 | -84 to -100 |
| All official holders | -198 | -4 to +84 |
| Private holders | +201 | +470 to +591 |
| All foreign holders | +3 | +466 to +675 |

![Change in foreign holdings of US Treasuries from September 2025 to September 2026, in $bn. Reported changes: Japan -126, China -135, official -198, private +201, total +3. Estimated net flows after removing a 5% to 7% valuation loss: Japan -53, China -92, official +40, private +531, total +571.](/post-assets/fed-last-hike-55-percent-by-march-2027-liquidity-2028-2026-11-25/foreign-treasury-holdings.en.png)

Both countries did sell. Japan's sales are tied to intervention in support of the yen (27.1 trillion yen in 2026), and after currency hedging the 10-year Treasury yields less than Japanese government bonds (2.24% against 2.66%). China has added to its gold reserves for 24 months in a row. Even so, their combined net sales of $124bn to $166bn are 6% to 8% of the roughly $2.1tn of net Treasury supply in fiscal 2027. Foreign holders as a whole were large net buyers.

## The bottleneck in Treasuries is who holds them

The buyers that replaced Japan and China are accounts in the United Kingdom, the Cayman Islands and Belgium, which means hedge funds and custody accounts. By SK Securities' count, price-sensitive holders own 70% of coupon securities, up from 45% in 2015. Long-term holders own 6.5%.

When the holders change, the same sale moves yields more. Using elasticities from a Fed paper, a $500bn sale raises the 10-year yield by 14bp if official holders sell and by 55bp if households and hedge funds do. Supply is absorbed in normal times, but in a shock the selling comes from one side. The indirect bid at the November 10-year auction fell from 80% to 61%, an early sign.

Brokerages attribute 52% to 83% of the 2026 rise in the 10-year yield to policy-rate expectations. On our own measure, using the Fed's Kim-Wright model, the term premium (the extra yield investors demand for holding long bonds) accounts for 43bp of the 95bp rise since the February low. In short, policy expectations explain more than half and the supply-and-demand premium explains 20% to 45%.

Will supply and demand stabilise? Our answer is that the supply gets absorbed, but price volatility is structurally higher. We expect the 10-year yield to trade between 4.4% and 5.6% in 2027 and to end the year around 4.8%. We put 45% on a break above 5.31% during 2027. A return to the low 4% range is unlikely without a credit accident.

## Central bank assets shrank, and private credit filled the gap

The combined assets of the Fed, the European Central Bank (ECB) and the Bank of Japan fell from $24.7tn at the end of 2021 to $17.2tn in November 2026. That is a 31% decline at market exchange rates and 26% at today's rates held constant. Half of the 2026 decline of 5.5% is a translation effect from the stronger dollar.

![Top: combined assets of the Fed, ECB and Bank of Japan in $tn from 2019 to 2026, at market exchange rates and at constant current rates, falling from an early-2022 peak to $17.2tn. Bottom: US M2 growth, year on year, recovering from negative in 2023 to +5.9% in 2026.](/post-assets/fed-last-hike-55-percent-by-march-2027-liquidity-2028-2026-11-25/cb-assets-m2.en.png)

Risk assets rose from 2022 to 2025 while that number fell. Central bank assets alone do not describe liquidity. We split it into four layers.

| Layer | Now | Direction |
|---|---|---|
| Settlement liquidity (dollar reserves) | $2.98tn, 9.03% of GDP. Reverse repo balance at zero | Tight. Debt-limit politics set the 2027 path |
| Central bank assets | Fed flat, ECB -3.7%, Bank of Japan -10.1% | Still shrinking |
| Private money and credit | M2 +5.9%, bank credit +6.7%, cross-border credit +11% | Expanding |
| Risk-asset liquidity | High-yield spreads near the year's high, bitcoin 51% below its peak | Signs of contraction |

For several years private credit filled the space left by shrinking public liquidity. The cushion that hid the process was $1.9tn sitting in the Fed's reverse repo facility, and that money is gone. From here, every dollar added to the Treasury's cash balance comes straight out of reserves.

## Debt-limit politics set the 2027 liquidity path

The statutory debt limit is $41.104tn. Debt subject to the limit was $40.528tn on November 23, leaving $575.6bn of room. Without action the limit binds between late January and March 2027. Republicans are pushing to raise it before the new Congress is seated, but Senate votes are uncertain.

Once the limit binds, the Treasury cannot borrow more and spends down its cash. Reserves rise while that cash flows into the banking system. When the limit is lifted, the Treasury rebuilds its balance and reserves fall.

![Bank reserves, actual from January 2025 to November 2026, and three projected paths. With the limit raised this year, reserves fall to $2.82tn in April 2027. If the limit binds in February and is resolved by June, reserves rise to about $3.3tn in summer and then fall. In a standoff into autumn they rise to $3.6tn in October and then drop sharply. All three paths converge near $2.9tn in early 2028.](/post-assets/fed-last-hike-55-percent-by-march-2027-liquidity-2028-2026-11-25/reserves-debt-limit-paths.en.png)

| Path | Probability | Liquidity in 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| Limit raised this year | 35% | Reserves fall to $2.88tn in January and $2.82tn in April. The Fed resumes bill purchases earlier, in the first half |
| Limit binds in February, resolved by June | 30% | $300bn to $400bn of Treasury cash is released from March to June, then absorbed again in summer |
| Limit binds in February, standoff into autumn | 35% | Reserves rise to $3.6tn in October, then about $720bn drains in the three months after a deal |

In all three paths reserves end up near $2.9tn in early 2028. The debt limit changes the route and adds no permanent liquidity. The paths share one feature. With no reverse repo cushion, reserves touch tight levels on every route, and the Fed is then likely to restart bill purchases. All four past balance-sheet expansions (October 2019, March 2020, March 2023, December 2025) were triggered by funding-market stress.

## Fixing the plumbing is different from easing

Treating "more liquidity" as one thing leads to reading it too early. We separate three things with different meanings. The figures are cumulative probabilities.

| What | By mid-2027 | By end-2027 | By mid-2028 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Settlement liquidity improves | 45% | 75% | 85% |
| Fed resumes bill purchases | 30% | 60% | 80% |
| Policy easing (first cut) | 5% | 25% | 60% |

Dollar liquidity in the banking system has about an even chance of improving by mid-2027 and a 75% chance by year-end. That is closer to fixing the plumbing. Fed bill purchases keep reserves adequate and do not ease policy. The liquidity that lifts asset prices comes with rate cuts, and those centre on the first half of 2028. For it to arrive in 2027, something has to break.

### Inflation and accidents give four paths

We define the inflation condition as above: core PCE at or below 2.75% on a three-month annualised basis with headline CPI below 3%, in the data through June 2027. We define an accident as a high-yield spread above 6%, or the overnight funding rate (SOFR) more than 15bp above the rate the Fed pays on reserves for a week, before the end of 2027.

| Path | Probability | Rates and liquidity | Chance of a 2027 cut |
|---|---|---|---|
| Inflation falls, no accident | 33% | Hikes end between December and the first quarter, a long hold, a cut in the first half of 2028 | 25% |
| Inflation falls, accident | 15% | An accident after the peak. Purchases and cuts in the second half of 2027. Risk assets fall first, then recover | 70% |
| Inflation too high, no accident | 30% | Hikes continue into the second or third quarter, to 4.75% or higher. The 10-year yield breaks 5.3% again | 3% |
| Inflation too high, accident | 22% | Rates stay high while liquidity tools are switched on. The worst mix for risk assets | 25% |

The accident paths add up to 37%. The most comfortable path, with inflation falling and no accident, is one in three.

## Equity investors should not read rising reserves as easing

From here on this is our interpretation.

First, if reserves rise in mid-2027 or the Fed starts buying bills, it is too early to call that the start of easing. The four past balance-sheet expansions moved separately from the rate cycle. What lowers discount rates is a rate cut, and in our base path that comes in 2028.

Second, the range for long yields is wide. A 10-year yield between 4.4% and 5.6% is a 120bp spread from bottom to top. Growth stocks, priced on distant earnings, feel that swing most. We take higher volatility, more than any particular level, as the working assumption.

Third, for AI infrastructure companies funding conditions matter before the level of rates. With 46% of the spending externally financed, we look first at reliance on outside funding and at debt maturities. In Korea, ten brokerages expect the Bank of Korea's terminal rate to be 3.50% (as of the day before its November 26 decision).

## Why this forecast could be wrong

The strongest counter-argument is that two energy shocks are the start of second-round effects. Core inflation is running at 3.75% on a three-month annualised basis, and the minutes describe broad price pressures. In that case the peak is near 5%.

Second, the peak may come from an accident, not from lower inflation (37%). Liquidity arrives sooner, but asset prices fall first. Third, a new closure of Hormuz erases the base effect in headline inflation. The fourth runs the other way. If AI investment drops and stocks correct, demand cools fast and December is the last hike (20%).

Three things hold in most scenarios. The spring fall and summer rebound in headline inflation were set by the 2026 oil path. Reserves are already tight, so the Fed is likely to resume purchases during 2027. Liquidity that comes with rate cuts belongs to 2028.

## Notes on the data

- Rates, prices, balance sheets and money supply come from 64 FRED series. October 2025 CPI was never published, so we interpolated it as the geometric mean of September and November. The October 2026 CPI rate relies on that value and is an estimate.
- The 2027 CPI path uses a regression of energy CPI on the change in Brent in the current and previous month, with a constant, and weights of 7.4% for energy, 13.4% for food and 79.2% for core. The pass-through from diesel to core inflation is weakly supported, so we show it only as a range.
- Fed funds futures are November 23 settlements as collected; we did not recheck them against the source. The net-flow estimates for foreign holdings depend on a duration assumption and cannot separate custody transfers from actual trades.
- The monthly path of Treasury cash after the debt limit binds is an assumption. Reserve paths assume no Fed purchases.
- We did not verify actual traffic through Hormuz, OPEC+ spare capacity or the original outlooks from global investment banks. Their forecasts come from press reports.
- The draft went through two independent adversarial reviews. We corrected the twelve points that held up against primary data, including the debt-limit headroom, the constant in the CPI model, the rewrite around core inflation, and the probability that the 10-year yield has peaked (from 65% to 50%).
- October PCE, the revised third-quarter GDP and the Bank of Korea's November 26 decision are not reflected here.
