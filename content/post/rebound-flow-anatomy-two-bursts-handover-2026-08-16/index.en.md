> Connecting context: [The Second Week of August in Review](/post/weekly-wrap-wallst-financing-reaction-shift-2026-08-14/) noted that foreign investors turned to four straight days of net buying starting Tuesday. That's accurate, but it's surface-level. This piece works from our own aggregation, breaking down eleven trading days of flow since the low by investor type, phase, and stock, to look at the structure underneath: who sold, who bought, and why buying at one moment differs in character from buying at another, even when both count as net buying.

## TL;DR

- The bottom line first. From August 10 to 14, the KOSPI rose from 6,258.77 to 6,977.94, <strong>up 11.49%, while retail investors net sold around 7 trillion won over the five sessions and foreign investors net bought in the mid-6 trillion won range</strong>. Institutions were modest net buyers. [Fact: exchange and media aggregation, totals by investor type vary by aggregation method] This rebound is a large-cap rally in which foreign investors absorbed the profit-taking supply that retail sold.
- But split the full period since the low (July 30) into phases, and foreign buying was not continuous. <strong>Foreigners bought in the 7 trillion won range in a single day on July 31, absorbing capitulation supply, then sold back in the 8 trillion won range during the early-August stall (August 3 to 11), and bought again in the 7 trillion won range starting August 12 once the inflation rally began</strong>. [Fact: own aggregation] It's an alternating structure: absorb the low's supply, sell back during the stall, buy again on the news rally.
- This structure carries a methodological lesson. Cut the window from August 1 to 14 instead, and you get <strong>the opposite conclusion: retail net buying, foreign net selling</strong>. That's because July 31 alone falls outside the window. Reading flow data is half about how you set the window.
- The rebound's buying returned to the exact same spot as the crash's selling. Since the low, the top foreign net-buying names have been Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, <strong>together accounting for half of total net buying</strong>, and the two stocks also made up roughly 78% of the single day of buying on July 31. That is an exact mirror image of how foreign selling concentrated in these same two names during the July crash. [Fact: own aggregation] It is flow-side evidence for this series' verdict that the decline was a positioning problem, not a fundamentals problem.
- The direction of the handover in these two stocks is healthy. From August 10 to 14, in Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix, <strong>retail investors sold 4.6 trillion won, while foreign investors absorbed 2.15 trillion won and institutions absorbed 2.66 trillion won</strong>. Retail net selling in these two stocks accounted for about 65% of total retail net selling across the KOSPI. [Fact: own aggregation]
- The caveats are just as clear. Foreign investors bought cash equities, but <strong>their cumulative August net-short position in futures was not fully unwound, and program trading was net selling</strong>. On the KOSDAQ, they were net sellers throughout the rebound. [Fact: own aggregation, public reporting confirms direction only] With cash strength, a futures hedge, and program-trading weakness all mixed together, it is too early to call this a full shift to risk-on.
- The remaining checks are whether foreign net buying in cash equities holds up cumulatively, whether the cumulative futures short position shrinks, whether non-arbitrage program trading flips to net buying, and whether foreign ownership ratios in the two stocks rise. The last of these is still unmet. Despite the heavy buying, Samsung Electronics' foreign ownership ratio is <strong>stuck at 46.70%, unchanged from the start of the month</strong>. [Fact: disclosure aggregation]
- One more variable to flag before Monday's open. Friday night, US retail sales fell 0.6% month over month, the largest miss versus consensus in 14 months, and the probability of a September hold rose into the 70% range. The US-Iran ceasefire extension remains at the agreement-in-principle stage past its expiration date, and Iran's foreign minister said the two sides are not in negotiations. [Fact: reporting]

<div class="thesis-callout">
<div class="thesis-callout__label">Key Framing</div>

Even when the net-buying total looks the same, when the buying happened changes what it means. On July 31, foreign investors absorbed the supply that forced liquidation dumped into the market. In early August, they took profit on that position, and from August 12, they bought again in response to news on inflation and earnings. Read this alternation as one continuous buying streak and you overstate the strength of the flow. Cut the window starting from August 1 and you read the direction as the exact opposite. What is confirmed now is the symmetry: the same two stocks sold in the crash were bought back by the same hands in the rebound, and the handover ran from retail to foreign and institutional investors. What is not confirmed is whether this buying reflects conviction that has already shed its hedge. Futures and program trading will answer that question first.

## 2. Split Into Phases: Buying That Came in Two Bursts

But the picture changes once you start from the low. On a Samsung Electronics closing-price basis, the low came on July 30 (207,000 won), and the stock rose 32.6% over the eleven trading days from there to August 14. Splitting flow by investor type across this full period into phases looks like this.

| Phase | Retail | Foreign | Detail |
|---|---|---|---|
| July 31 (single day) | -8.3 trillion won | +7.4 trillion won | The largest rebound day on record. After forced liquidation, foreign investors absorbed the capitulation supply. Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix made up about 78% of that day's buying |
| August 3 to 11 | +9.9 trillion won | -8.9 trillion won | Seven-session stall. Foreign investors took profit while retail bought the dip. Direction reversed |
| August 12 to 14 | -7.7 trillion won | +7.7 trillion won | Three-day inflation rally. The reaction function shifted, and the investor types reversed again |

Foreign buying was not a continuous flow across the eleven trading days. It was <strong>two concentrated bursts of buying with one round of selling back in between</strong>. The first burst was the single day of July 31, and the second was the three days from August 12. In the seven trading days in between, foreigners sold back roughly as much as they had bought in the first burst, and retail investors took the other side. Absorb supply at the low, trim it during the stall, then come back in once direction is confirmed: this alternation is a textbook flow pattern that has repeated across first rallies off a bottom.

This breakdown carries a methodological lesson. Cut the window from August 1 to 14, and the numbers come out to roughly +2.2 trillion won in retail net buying and -1.2 trillion won in foreign net selling, <strong>the exact opposite of the surface verdict</strong>. That's because it excludes the single day of July 31. With flow data, where you cut the calendar decides half the conclusion. One person could say "foreigners sold in August," and another could say "foreigners drove the rebound," and both could be arithmetically true. The window used for judgment should follow the structure of events (the low, the stall, the rally), not the calendar.

## 3. Buying That Returned to the Same Spot

What foreigners bought is the second finding. Since the low, the top foreign net-buying names have been Samsung Electronics (about 3.1 trillion won) and SK Hynix (about 2.6 trillion won), and <strong>the two stocks account for about 48% of total net buying</strong>. Broaden the semiconductor group to include SK Square and Samsung Electronics preferred shares, and the share tops half. That's an exact mirror image of how foreign selling concentrated almost entirely in these same two stocks during the July crash. [Fact: own aggregation]

This symmetry tells us something. This series has judged the July decline as a positioning problem, not an information problem. If the same investor type sold and bought the same two stocks, it is more consistent to read this as <strong>a position being unwound and then restored</strong> than as a fundamental reassessment happening in between. Because the reason for selling was not fundamentals, the reason for buying didn't wait for confirmation that fundamentals had improved either.

Looking at the handover in these two stocks by investor type over the five days makes the direction even clearer.

As a direction of handover, this is healthy.

It should also be noted that handovers ran in the opposite direction too. Samsung Electro-Mechanics, which ranked among the top retail net-buying names (retail +0.38 trillion won against foreign -0.84 trillion won), and some small and mid-cap semiconductor stocks overlap with the top foreign net-selling names. And on the KOSDAQ, foreign investors were net sellers of about 1.7 trillion won throughout the rebound, with institutions and retail taking that supply. [Fact: own aggregation] Foreign investors' August was a rotation out of the KOSDAQ and the periphery and into large-cap semiconductors, not a broader expansion of bets across the Korean market as a whole.

## 4. The Caveat: Cash Was Bought, the Hedge Remains

The reason it's hard to call this buying a full shift to risk-on lies on the derivatives side.

By our own aggregation, foreign net buying in KOSPI 200 futures came to only 1,462 contracts from August 10 to 14, and the cumulative net-short position for the month, including the August 3 sell-off day, still stands at just over 14,000 contracts. Program trading over the same period was net selling of about 1.1 trillion won. [Fact: own aggregation, derivatives and program-trading data. Public reporting does not confirm the contract count] Media analysis points the same way: foreign investors are buying cash equities in size while combining it with futures selling to partially hedge market risk, and over the past month this pattern of flipping positions worth several trillion won on a single-day basis has repeated. [Fact: media analysis]

So the character of this buying doesn't reduce to one thing. Foreign investors picked out and bought the large-cap names that had fallen the most, while offloading part of the index risk through futures, and in the process absorbed retail's profit-taking and leverage-unwind supply. That's different from a phase where passive money sweeps up the entire market.

Here is what needs to be confirmed before we can call this a full return to trend.

| Confirmation condition | Current status |
|---|---|
| Cumulative foreign net buying in cash equities holds up | In progress. Three straight days in the 2 trillion won range since the 12th |
| Cumulative monthly KOSPI 200 futures short shrinks | Not met. Cumulative net-short position remains |
| Non-arbitrage program trading flips to net buying | Not met. Net selling for the week |
| Foreign ownership ratio rises in Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix | Not met. Samsung Electronics at 46.70%, unchanged from the start of the month |

[Fact: own aggregation and disclosures. Ownership ratio as of August 14]

The last line may be surprising. Foreign investors bought in the trillions of won over five days, yet Samsung Electronics' foreign ownership ratio sits at 46.70%, unchanged from the start of the month. This could be a technical factor, such as an aggregation lag or a change in shares outstanding, but the cause is unverified. [Blocked: cause unverified] The point at which the ownership ratio is confirmed to actually rise will be the strongest evidence that this buying is a restoration of holdings, not short-term trading.

Confirming that the three follow-up indicators, futures, program trading, and the ownership ratio, shed that hedge is the task for reading the next phase.
