Korea can be a small allocation in a global portfolio and a major position in an emerging-markets portfolio at the same time. It can also be a concentrated semiconductor bet inside either mandate. Those are three different facts, and they matter when investors try to interpret a sharp move in Korean stocks.

As of November 5, 2026, South Korea represented 2.06% of the iShares MSCI ACWI ETF and 18.28% of the iShares MSCI Emerging Markets ETF (EEM). The ratio is 8.87 times. These are two fund portfolios, not universal weights for all global or emerging-markets investors. The fund weights are from November 5; fund net assets cited below are from November 6.

The difference reflects the mandate. A global equity fund spreads exposure across developed and emerging markets. An emerging-markets fund excludes developed markets, so its remaining countries have larger weights. MSCI classifies Korea as emerging; FTSE Russell classifies it as developed. A FTSE developed-markets fund can therefore own Korean shares while a comparable MSCI World fund excludes them. [MSCI’s June 2026 classification review](https://ir.msci.com/news-releases/news-release-details/msci-announces-results-msci-2026-market-classification-review) describes unresolved accessibility concerns. [FTSE Russell’s October 2026 classification paper](https://research.ftserussell.com/products/downloads/FTSE_Equity_Country_Classification_Paper.pdf) lists South Korea as developed.

The fund weights also need context. On November 6, ACWI had $31.495 billion in net assets and EEM had $27.513 billion. Multiplying those values by each fund’s November 5 Korea allocation gives approximate exposures of $649 million and $5.029 billion. This one-day-misaligned calculation is not a reported holdings value. It illustrates how a smaller fund can allocate more dollars to Korea when its mandate gives Korea a larger share.

MSCI’s EM index page reports Korea at 21.4% as of September 30, while EEM’s issuer exposure shows 18.28% on November 5. The dates and measures differ, so this is not a same-day comparison. EEM is passive; its weight does not represent every active EM manager. [MSCI](https://www.msci.com/indexes/index/891800/msci-em-emerging-markets-index-2?tab=document-downloads) and [iShares](https://www.ishares.com/us/products/239637/ishares-msci-emerging-markets-etf) show the respective date-stamped data.

## The mandate determines whether Korea is even eligible

Investors do not all respond to a lower Korean share price in the same way. Some mandates cannot buy Korean shares directly. Others own them because the benchmark requires it. Still others may reduce risk without changing their view of a particular company.

| Mandate or strategy | Korea’s role | What can change its weight | Possible behavior during a sharp decline |
|---|---|---|---|
| U.S. domestic equities | Usually outside the direct investment mandate | Eligible markets and listing rules | A lower Korean valuation does not make the fund an automatic buyer. Overseas sales exposure is not the same as direct Korean ownership. |
| Global equities or pension mandates | A small country allocation, with access to selected large companies | Expected returns, country and sector limits, total-portfolio allocation | A regional cut may reduce Korea exposure. A long-term investor may instead rebalance toward its target weight. |
| MSCI emerging-markets active | A large benchmark country allocation and a source of semiconductor exposure | Relative prospects versus Taiwan, China and India; active risk limits | A full exit can create substantial benchmark-relative risk. A manager can still underweight Korea if its outlook worsens. |
| Market-cap-weighted index | Securities held to track the index | Fund subscriptions and redemptions, index changes and rebalancing | A price decline alone does not require a sale. Korea’s weight can fall relative to other markets while the fund keeps the same shares. |
| Global technology or semiconductor strategy | A route to memory, HBM and capital-spending exposure | Product competitiveness, earnings estimates, customer spending and relative valuation | The strategy may cut industry exposure before country exposure, or differentiate between companies. |
| Macro, trend-following or volatility-target strategy | Exposure through equities, index futures or currency positions | Trends, volatility, risk limits and margin | The strategy may reduce positions even if its long-term investment view has not changed. Not every such strategy trades Korea directly. |
| Long-short or relative-value fund | A company or sector position, sometimes paired with another market | Gross exposure, stop-losses, redemptions and borrowing costs | A Korean sale may be paired with a purchase elsewhere. It does not necessarily signal a bearish view on Korea as a whole. |
| Value, activist or Korea-focused fund | Security-specific opportunities | Shareholder returns, governance, catalysts and cash flows | It may buy against the market, but still faces redemptions and liquidity limits. |

These are possible behaviors inferred from mandate design, not observed orders. Pension funds and sovereign wealth funds are asset owners; passive, active and risk-targeted are investment approaches. One owner can use several approaches. A manager’s home country does not identify its mandate.

## A large pool of assets does not mean an equally large Korean position

Total asset manager AUM is not the right number to multiply by Korea’s index weight. First identify the assets in the relevant equity mandate. Then apply that account’s benchmark, permitted range and Korea allocation. A manager with many clients is not one investor making one decision.

The U.S. Treasury’s year-end 2025 survey found $360 billion in U.S. holdings of Korean equities, out of $15.232 trillion in foreign equities, or 2.36%. The denominator excludes U.S. domestic equities. The survey covers U.S. residents, not just U.S. institutions; fund domicile and custody can affect country attribution. [U.S. Treasury survey, released October 30, 2026](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/sb0672).

The reverse picture is also important. Foreign investors held ₩2,296.0 trillion of Korean listed shares, or 34.8% of market capitalization, at the end of September 2026, according to the Financial Supervisory Service figures reproduced by KDI. Their net sales during that month were ₩19.116 trillion. Holdings are a stock measured at a date; net sales are a flow during a period. The figures must not be combined as if they were the same measure. [KDI posting of FSS data](https://eiec.kdi.re.kr/policy/materialView.do?num=288831).

This is the asymmetry: Korea can be a small slice of a global portfolio while foreign ownership remains a large part of Korea’s market. Ownership share alone does not measure trading power or prove price impact. But it helps explain why changes in global allocations can matter to local prices.

Consider a scale illustration. Assume an account has $1 billion and invests 1% in one stock. At an assumed exchange rate of ₩1,400 per dollar, $10 million equals ₩14 billion. If the account has $100 billion, the same 1% allocation is $1 billion, or ₩1.4 trillion.

Assume the stock trades ₩10 billion per day and the account limits orders to 10% of turnover, or ₩1 billion per day. The smaller position would take 14 trading days to build; the larger, 1,400 days. This simplified example assumes constant prices and volume, no competing orders and no market impact. It is not a universal participation limit. It shows why large accounts may focus on liquid shares, futures or ETFs, or choose a smaller target weight.

A second illustration uses ACWI’s 2.06% Korea weight. If a hypothetical $100 billion global equity account cut that weight by 20%, it would sell about $412 million, or ₩576.8 billion at the assumed exchange rate. That is a 0.412 percentage-point change in the account, not a forecast of an actual sale or its market impact.

## The country index is also a concentrated industry position

The MSCI Korea Index’s October 30, 2026 composition shows Samsung Electronics common shares at 35.54% and SK hynix at 26.45%. Together they account for 61.99%. Samsung Electronics preferred shares add another 4.72%, bringing the three securities to 66.71%. Information Technology accounts for 70.76% of the index. These are weights in MSCI’s large- and mid-cap index, not weights in all KOSPI and KOSDAQ companies. [MSCI Korea Index, live index data](https://www.msci.com/indexes/index/941000/msci-korea-index).

If Samsung common and SK hynix each fell 10% while all other constituents stayed flat, their direct contribution would be about −6.20 percentage points. This static sensitivity is not an actual-day attribution. It shows why a few semiconductor stocks can drive the index while many companies are unchanged.

Buying Korean equities therefore brings meaningful exposure to memory and semiconductor earnings. Those earnings respond to prices, volumes and capital spending. A global investor already holding a large technology or semiconductor position may not see a Korea allocation as much diversification. Strategic importance to the technology supply chain does not make the associated shares a defensive asset.

## Volatility can be much higher than the global benchmark

![Ten-year annualized volatility: Korea 33.12%, emerging markets 17.68%, ACWI IMI 14.96%. Monthly net returns in USD through October 30, 2026.](/post-assets/global-capital-korea-small-allocation-large-volatility-2026-11-08/volatility.en.png)

MSCI’s index risk figures are based on monthly net returns through October 30, 2026. The figures below are annualized standard deviations, not daily realized volatility or forecasts. Returns are in U.S. dollars and reflect reinvested net dividends.

| Measure | Korea | Emerging markets | ACWI IMI |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 3-year annualized volatility | 47.80% | 17.88% | 12.02% |
| 5-year annualized volatility | 41.94% | 18.79% | 14.99% |
| 10-year annualized volatility | 33.12% | 17.68% | 14.96% |
| 2024 return | −23.40% | +7.50% | +16.37% |
| 2025 return | +99.85% | +33.57% | +22.06% |
| October 2026 return | −15.56% | −8.05% | −1.56% |

On this sample, Korea’s 10-year volatility was 2.21 times ACWI IMI’s, and its 3-year volatility was 3.98 times as high. These ratios are not betas and should not be treated as constants. They include large rallies as well as declines. Korea’s 2025 gain is a counterexample to the claim that global investors always avoid the market. Its 2024 fall while global equities rose also shows that not every Korean decline can be explained by global risk aversion alone. Company earnings and valuations matter too. All three series in this table appear in the same [MSCI Korea USD net-return factsheet dated October 30, 2026](https://www.msci.com/documents/10199/255599/msci-korea-index-net.pdf).

For an unhedged dollar investor, the Korean won changes the result. Let X be the won price of one dollar:

`1 + dollar return = (1 + local-asset return) × (X at the start / X at the end)`

The MSCI Korea Index returned −12.44% in won in 2024 and −23.40% in dollars, a difference of about 10.96 percentage points. In October 2026 it returned −16.76% in won and −15.56% in dollars, so the currency slightly reduced the dollar loss that month. A weaker won does not explain every equity decline. A fund may hedge its currency risk, and sale proceeds are not always converted immediately into dollars. [MSCI Korea in won](https://www.msci.com/documents/10199/255599/msci-korea-index-krw-net.pdf) and [in dollars](https://www.msci.com/indexes/index/941000/msci-korea-index).

Capital flows also differ by asset class. The Bank of Korea reported that in 2025 foreign equity flows were −$7.1 billion while bond flows were +$49.1 billion. Much of the bond investment used swaps, so it does not translate directly into spot-market foreign-exchange demand. “Money leaving Korea” must be separated into equity, bond and currency flows. [Bank of Korea discussion](https://www.bok.or.kr/portal/bbs/B0000347/view.do?menuNo=201106&nttId=10095828).

## How risk limits and liquidity may amplify a decline

Some strategies maintain a relatively stable risk budget. In a simple volatility-target model, position size varies roughly in inverse proportion to expected volatility. If expected volatility doubles from 20% to 40%, the same risk budget could imply half the position. Real multi-asset portfolios also consider correlations, hedges, loss limits and funding. This is an explanatory model, not a claim that every long-term institution follows the rule.

Daily leveraged funds provide another simplified example. Suppose a fund has net assets of 100 and exposure of 200, or twice its assets. If the underlying market falls 10%, assets fall to 80 while exposure falls to 180. To return to twice net assets, the fund would need exposure of 160, requiring a further reduction of 20. This assumes linear exposure, no new flows and no costs. It does not estimate actual ETF sales during a Korean selloff.

Index futures can also interact with cash shares. If futures trade below fair value, a relative-value trade may buy futures and sell a basket of shares. But a negative futures basis alone does not prove that arbitrage trading is occurring or that it is driving market pressure. Fair value depends on interest, dividends, time to expiry and transaction costs.

When prices fall, volatility rises and trading liquidity weakens, some portfolios may reduce risk. That can add to selling pressure and create a feedback loop. Market capitalization is not a pool of cash waiting to buy shares. A marginal transaction price changes the marked value of all outstanding shares, so net sales and the decline in market capitalization are not one-for-one measures.

## What the August 2024 selloff does—and does not—show

On August 5, 2024, the KOSPI fell 8.77% and the KOSDAQ fell 11.30%. Foreign investors sold ₩1.5282 trillion net on the KOSPI that day. The TOPIX fell about 12% on its local trading date, while the S&P 500 fell 3.0% on its August 5 close. These are local closing returns, not reactions measured at the same time. The U.S. close came after the Korean market had closed, so it cannot be treated as an earlier cause of the Korean session. [Korean Financial Services Commission release](https://www.fsc.go.kr/po010101/82809) and [BIS analysis](https://www.bis.org/publications/bulletin-90-market-turbulence-and-carry-trade-unwind-august-2024.pdf).

BIS linked the turbulence to a U.S. growth-data shock, leveraged positions, an unwind of yen carry trades and higher margin requirements. Its rough central estimate of the carry-trade size was about $250 billion, with substantial data gaps. That amount was neither Korean investment nor money withdrawn from Korea.

The episode supports the possibility that common shocks and forced risk reduction can magnify market moves. The larger fall in Japan also argues against a Korea-only explanation. The ₩1.5282 trillion foreign net sale is a same-day observation. It does not identify which strategies sold or how much of the market decline they caused.

When another selloff comes, compare the shock’s timing with market hours; normalize foreign net sales by market turnover; separate large semiconductor stocks from the rest of the index; and check futures, ETF share creation and redemption, domestic leverage, earnings estimates, currency and credit spreads. Foreign net sales, program trading and estimated ETF redemptions may describe overlapping transactions. Do not add them together as “total outflows.”

The evidence supports a clear distinction. Korea’s role varies by mandate: a small allocation in global equities, a much larger allocation in MSCI emerging-markets products, and a developed-market holding in FTSE-based developed-market products. A high foreign ownership share can make global decisions consequential locally, but it does not prove who is selling or why. Concentration, currency moves, volatility limits and liquidity can amplify a decline. They do not establish that every selloff is mechanical, that foreign investors know more, or that a rebound is due.

MSCI’s June review identified execution frictions: offshore won non-deliverability, insufficient extended-hours FX liquidity, limited omnibus-account and in-kind-transfer adoption, and compliance and prefunding burdens. The review documents institutional concerns but does not quantify a valuation discount attributable to them. [MSCI accessibility assessment](https://ir.msci.com/news-releases/news-release-details/msci-announces-results-msci-2026-market-classification-review).

Our interpretation would weaken if semiconductor concentration fell persistently, same-currency volatility converged toward global peers, or actual order and redemption data showed that company-specific information explained a particular decline better than mechanical risk reduction. A selloff with stable earnings expectations has different recovery conditions from one accompanied by lasting earnings cuts.
